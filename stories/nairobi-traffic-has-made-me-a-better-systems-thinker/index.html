Nairobi Traffic Has Made Me a Better Systems Thinker

The involuntary meditation session

There's a specific kind of thinking that only happens when you have no phone signal, no podcast, no way out, and two hours of stationary traffic ahead of you. Nairobi delivers this experience reliably, usually on Mombasa Road, usually when you have somewhere to be.

Last Tuesday I had a notepad. I filled four pages. This post is the parts worth keeping.

On bottlenecks

Nairobi traffic is a masterclass in how a single bottleneck — one broken traffic light, one matatu that stopped at a bad angle — cascades into a city-wide slowdown two hours later. The cause and the effect are so separated in time and space that most people never connect them.

Software systems do exactly the same thing. The slow query from this morning is the timeout error your users see this afternoon. The missing index is the 3am alert. Understanding the cascade is the whole game.

On the illusion of control

Sitting in traffic, you have two choices: fight it (change lanes constantly, honk, stress) or accept it (pick a lane, turn off the engine if it's bad enough, think). The fighters arrive at roughly the same time as the acceptors. They just arrive angrier.

I've started applying this to project timelines. Some delays are traffic. You can fight them and arrive stressed, or route around them early and arrive ready to work.

The matatu that cuts you off is never going to change. Build your mental model around what's actually variable.
