The Gig Economy: A Consultant's Honest Take

I've been in IT consultancy for over 10 years. It didn't start that way — that's a story for another day — but that's where I've landed. And one of the biggest shifts in how I and many others like me work is something that now has an official name: the gig economy.

Before the buzzword, though, there's a simpler truth. You spend years getting really good at something. You hone it, refine it, occasionally give it away for free just to prove you can do it. Slowly, people start associating your name with that skill. That's how consultants are made — not overnight, and not by accident.

So what exactly is the gig economy?

At its core, it's simple: short-term work, agreed terms, monetary compensation. Google calls it "a labour market characterized by the prevalence of short-term contracts or freelance work as opposed to permanent jobs." Wikipedia will tell you about independent contractors and on-demand platforms. My version? You have a skill, someone needs it for a defined period, they pay you for it. That's the gig.

What makes it an economy is scale. As more industries go digital, demand for professional services — often specific, short-term, and highly skilled — has exploded. Someone doesn't need a full-time IT department; they need their system fixed. Someone doesn't need a permanent designer; they need a website. The work is real, the expertise required is real, and the market for it keeps growing.

Finding the work

Here's the part nobody glamorizes enough: getting gigs largely comes down to who you know. Word of mouth remains the most reliable pipeline, especially early on. You do good work, someone mentions your name, and that mention leads to the next one.

Platforms like LinkedIn, Upwork, Fiverr, Freelancer, and locally, Kuhustle, have made it easier to get in front of clients you'd never meet organically. But don't mistake access for traction — building a credible profile and earning trust on these platforms takes time, just like it does in real life.

Why it works

The gig economy suits people who value autonomy. You choose what to take on, when to take it on, and what you're willing to accept as compensation. That flexibility is genuinely valuable — it lets you balance your workload against your life, not the other way around.

Negotiation is also part of the package, and that's a good thing. Every engagement is consciously agreed upon — scope, timeline, payment. When it's done right, both sides know exactly what they're getting into.

And then there's the network. It can feel like a slow build, but a trusted professional network is one of the most valuable things you can have in this line of work. Each gig, done well, is a reference. Each client, treated right, is a potential introduction to the next one.

Where it gets hard

The same things that make the gig economy attractive also make it unforgiving when you get them wrong.

Scoping is one of the trickiest skills to develop. Underestimating the time and effort a gig requires — especially a complex one — can turn a profitable engagement into a frustrating one quickly. Clients don't always know how to articulate what they need, and it falls on you to ask the right questions upfront. Getting that information early isn't just good practice; it's what separates a smooth project from a messy one.

Building trust also takes longer than most people expect. If you're new to the space, that initial period of proving yourself — without an established reputation to lean on — is genuinely tough. There's no shortcut for it, only consistency.

The bottom line

The gig economy works. Not for everyone, and not without effort — but for people who are serious about their craft, willing to network, and disciplined about how they manage their work, it can be a genuinely sustainable way to operate.

What makes it work, at least in my experience, isn't just the skill. It's the trust. Showing up on time, listening carefully, delivering what you promised, and occasionally caring about the relationship beyond the invoice. That's what gets you the next gig — and the one after that.
